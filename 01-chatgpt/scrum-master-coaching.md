# 🧭 Scrum Master Coaching Experiment: Using ChatGPT to Support Team Coaching

## 📌 1. Scenario

A Scrum Master is supporting a Scrum Team that is facing challenges such as low participation, dependency issues, recurring impediments, or difficulty taking ownership of improvements.

The Scrum Master wants to explore whether ChatGPT can help prepare for coaching conversations and identify useful questions, approaches, and coaching techniques.

## 🎯 2. Objective

Explore how ChatGPT can support a Scrum Master in coaching by:

- Helping prepare for difficult coaching conversations
- Suggesting powerful, open-ended coaching questions
- Exploring different approaches to common team challenges
- Helping the Scrum Master challenge assumptions and biases
- Identifying possible coaching techniques to try
- Supporting reflection on the Scrum Master's own approach

The objective is not to let AI coach the team directly or provide a fixed solution. The Scrum Master should use AI as a preparation and reflection tool while adapting the approach to the team's actual context.

## 🤖 3. AI Prompt — Scrum Master Coaching

> Act as an experienced Agile Coach and Scrum Master Coach.
>
> A Scrum Master is supporting a Scrum Team that is experiencing the following challenges:
>
> - Some team members rarely participate during Sprint Retrospectives.
> - The team depends heavily on the Scrum Master to resolve impediments.
> - The same improvement actions keep appearing in Retrospectives without meaningful follow-through.
> - Developers sometimes wait for the Scrum Master to coordinate with other teams instead of taking ownership.
>
> Analyze this situation from a coaching perspective.
>
> Identify:
> 1. Possible underlying causes to explore
> 2. Coaching questions the Scrum Master could ask
> 3. Different coaching approaches the Scrum Master could consider
> 4. How the Scrum Master could encourage team ownership and self-management
> 5. What the Scrum Master should avoid doing
> 6. How the Scrum Master could reflect on their own behavior and potential contribution to the situation
>
> Do not assume that the team is unwilling or incapable. Consider different possible contexts and avoid immediately prescribing solutions.

## 💬 4. AI Response — Scrum Master Coaching Analysis
Absolutely. The key coaching principle here is: **don’t interpret these behaviors as “the team is not self-managing” before understanding why they are happening.** The Scrum Master should investigate the system, team dynamics, organizational environment, and their own behavior.

## 1. Possible underlying causes to explore

### A. Low participation in Retrospectives

Possible causes include:

* Team members may not feel psychologically safe to speak openly.
* Previous suggestions may have been ignored, creating a sense that “nothing changes anyway.”
* The retrospective format may have become repetitive or overly Scrum-Master-driven.
* Some members may be naturally quieter and prefer written/anonymous input.
* There may be unresolved conflict or tension within the team.
* The team may not clearly understand the purpose of the retrospective.
* Remote/distributed working may make spontaneous participation harder.
* Team members may feel that raising problems could create blame or conflict.

**Don't conclude:** “They are disengaged.”

Instead ask: **“What is making participation difficult or less valuable for them?”**

---

### B. Heavy dependence on the Scrum Master for impediments

Possible causes:

* The team may have become accustomed to the Scrum Master acting as the primary problem solver.
* Team members may not know which impediments they can resolve themselves.
* Organizational processes may genuinely require someone with the Scrum Master's access or authority.
* External dependencies may be difficult to navigate.
* Developers may have previously tried to resolve issues and received little support.
* The Scrum Master may unintentionally be reinforcing dependency by stepping in too quickly.

This distinction is important:

> **Supporting the team ≠ doing everything for the team.**

---

### C. Repeated retrospective actions

This could indicate:

* Actions are too vague: “Improve communication.”
* Nobody explicitly owns the action.
* The action is unrealistic within the team's current environment.
* The team has too many improvement actions.
* There is no mechanism to inspect progress.
* The underlying problem hasn't actually been understood.
* The action addresses a symptom rather than the root cause.
* The team doesn't have the authority needed to make the change.

For example:

> “Improve collaboration with Team X”

may repeatedly appear because the real issue could be unclear ownership, an organizational dependency, an architectural constraint, or lack of an agreed communication mechanism.

---

### D. Developers expecting the Scrum Master to coordinate with other teams

Again, don't immediately label this as lack of ownership.

Explore:

* Do developers have access to the other team?
* Are they empowered to negotiate dependencies?
* Has the organization historically expected Scrum Masters to coordinate cross-team work?
* Are there political or hierarchical barriers?
* Does the other team respond only to managers/Scrum Masters?
* Does the team understand that developers can directly collaborate with others?
* Has the Scrum Master unintentionally created a pattern where they always become the intermediary?

---

# 2. Coaching questions the Scrum Master could ask

The questions should help the team **discover the situation themselves**, rather than leading them toward a predetermined answer.

### About Retrospectives

Instead of:

> “Why aren't you participating?”

Try:

* “How useful are our retrospectives to you today?”
* “What would make these conversations more valuable?”
* “What makes it easy or difficult for you to speak openly here?”
* “Are there topics we're avoiding?”
* “What happens when someone raises an uncomfortable issue?”
* “What would make you feel safer contributing?”
* “How could we change the retrospective format?”

### About impediments

* “What impediments do you believe the team can resolve without me?”
* “Where do you need my help, and where could you take the lead?”
* “What have you already tried?”
* “Who is best positioned to address this?”
* “What is preventing you from approaching that person directly?”
* “What would you need from me to take this forward yourselves?”

Notice the difference:

> **“I'll contact them.”**

versus

> **“What would you like to try first, and how can I support you?”**

---

### About repeated improvement actions

* “We've discussed this several times. What do we think is keeping it from changing?”
* “Are we addressing the root cause or just the symptom?”
* “What have we learned since the last time we discussed this?”
* “Is this action still relevant?”
* “What would success look like?”
* “What is one small experiment we could try?”
* “What can we realistically change before the next retrospective?”

---

### About self-management

Questions such as:

* “Who is closest to this problem?”
* “What decision can you make without waiting for me?”
* “What information do you need to make that decision?”
* “What would you do if I weren't available?”
* “What support do you actually need from me?”
* “What responsibility do you want to take for this?”

These questions gradually shift the conversation from **dependency → ownership**.

---

# 3. Different coaching approaches to consider

There isn't one correct coaching technique for every situation.

### Approach 1: Facilitative coaching

The Scrum Master asks questions and helps the team discover their own solutions.

Useful when:

* The team has sufficient knowledge.
* The issue is primarily ownership or decision-making.
* The Scrum Master suspects they may be solving too much for the team.

---

### Approach 2: Teaching

Sometimes the team genuinely lacks knowledge.

For example, the Scrum Master might explain:

* What self-management means in Scrum.
* The purpose of a retrospective.
* How to formulate meaningful improvement experiments.
* How teams can handle dependencies.
* How to distinguish an impediment from something the team can solve themselves.

**Coaching isn't always just asking questions.**

A Scrum Master may teach when there is a genuine knowledge gap.

---

### Approach 3: Mentoring

The Scrum Master can share experience without taking ownership away from the team.

For example:

> “I've seen teams handle cross-team dependencies by having the developer closest to the dependency establish the initial conversation. Would you like to explore whether something similar could work here?”

The team still decides.

---

### Approach 4: Facilitation intervention

If retrospectives have become predictable, the Scrum Master might change the format.

For example:

* Silent brainstorming first.
* Anonymous input.
* Different retrospective techniques.
* Small-group discussion.
* Dot voting.
* Root-cause exploration.
* One improvement experiment rather than a long action list.

The objective isn't to make retrospectives entertaining.

It's to create conditions where **useful inspection and adaptation can happen.**

---

### Approach 5: Systemic coaching

If the same problem repeatedly returns, look beyond the team.

Ask:

> “Is this actually a team problem, or is the team operating inside a system that makes this behavior inevitable?”

For example, repeated cross-team dependency issues could originate from:

* organizational structure,
* unclear ownership,
* architecture,
* release processes,
* management practices,
* resource constraints,
* external approvals.

A Scrum Master should avoid trying to “coach away” a systemic problem.

---

# 4. Encouraging team ownership and self-management

A useful progression is:

**Do → Demonstrate → Support → Coach → Step back**

For example, suppose another team's dependency blocks a developer.

### Dependency 1

Scrum Master handles everything.

### Dependency 2

Scrum Master says:

> “Who from our team could initiate the conversation?”

Developer takes the lead.

Scrum Master remains available.

### Dependency 3

Developer handles the conversation independently.

Scrum Master only observes/supports if needed.

That gradually builds capability.

---

### Make ownership visible

Instead of:

> “Scrum Master will follow up with Team X.”

Try:

> “Developer A will initiate the discussion with Team X. Scrum Master will help escalate if organizational support is required.”

This distinction is powerful.

The Scrum Master becomes an **enabler**, not the team's coordinator.

---

### Create smaller improvement experiments

Instead of five retrospective actions, the team might select one meaningful experiment.

For example:

> **Problem:** Cross-team dependencies are discovered late.

> **Experiment:** For the next Sprint, developers identify external dependencies during refinement and contact the relevant team before Sprint Planning.

> **Success indicator:** Fewer dependency-related surprises during the Sprint.

Then inspect the result in the next retrospective.

This creates a feedback loop:

**Problem → Hypothesis → Experiment → Observe → Learn → Adapt**

rather than:

**Problem → Action item → Forget → Repeat**

---

# 5. What the Scrum Master should avoid

### ❌ Becoming the team's permanent problem solver

If the Scrum Master repeatedly says:

> “I'll handle it.”

the team can unintentionally learn:

> “Why should we handle it?”

---

### ❌ Forcing participation

Don't pressure quiet team members with:

> “Everyone must speak during the retrospective.”

Instead explore why participation is low and experiment with different ways of contributing.

---

### ❌ Publicly labeling people as disengaged

Avoid:

> “Developers aren't taking ownership.”

Use observable behavior:

> “I've noticed that several dependency conversations are being routed through me. I'd like us to understand why.”

---

### ❌ Creating retrospective action-item overload

Ten actions often become zero actions.

Prioritize meaningful experiments.

---

### ❌ Taking ownership away from Developers

The Scrum Master should not automatically become:

* dependency coordinator,
* project administrator,
* task assigner,
* escalation manager,
* communication proxy.

---

### ❌ Assuming every problem is a team problem

Some problems require organizational change.

Self-management doesn't mean:

> “The team must solve everything themselves.”

It means the team should have appropriate autonomy while the Scrum Master helps address impediments and organizational barriers.

---

# 6. Scrum Master's self-reflection

This is probably the **most important part of the situation.**

The Scrum Master should honestly ask:

### “Am I contributing to the dependency?”

For example:

* Do I jump in before the team has a chance to respond?
* Do I answer questions that the team could answer themselves?
* Do I coordinate every external dependency?
* Do people come to me because I have trained them to?
* Do I measure my effectiveness by how many problems I personally solve?
* Do I feel uncomfortable allowing the team to struggle with a problem?
* Am I facilitating retrospectives so strongly that the team's conversation becomes my conversation?
* Do I follow up on every action instead of helping the team establish its own accountability mechanism?
* Am I protecting the team from organizational problems instead of helping the team learn to navigate them?
* Am I accidentally creating a **Scrum Master → Team dependency**?

A particularly powerful question is:

> **“If I disappeared for two weeks, what would this team still be able to do without me?”**

That isn't about making the Scrum Master unnecessary.

It's about measuring whether the Scrum Master's work is **building team capability**.

---

# Overall coaching stance

I would approach this team with **curiosity rather than judgment**.

The Scrum Master's mindset could be:

> **“I see some patterns that suggest dependency and limited follow-through. Before deciding what to change, I want to understand what is creating these patterns.”**

Then:

**Observe → Explore → Understand → Experiment → Inspect → Adapt**

The ultimate goal isn't to make the Scrum Master less involved simply for the sake of it.

The goal is to gradually move from:

**“The Scrum Master makes things happen.”**

to:

**“The team is increasingly capable of making things happen, while the Scrum Master creates the conditions that enable them.”**

That is a much stronger demonstration of **coaching for self-management** in a Scrum Master interview or real-world situation.

## ✅ 5. What AI Did Well

- AI avoided immediately labeling the team as disengaged or unwilling to self-manage.
- It explored multiple possible causes behind each observed behavior.
- It provided practical, open-ended coaching questions instead of prescribing immediate solutions.
- It distinguished between coaching, teaching, mentoring, facilitation, and systemic coaching.
- It emphasized gradually building team ownership rather than simply removing the Scrum Master from the process.
- It highlighted the Scrum Master's potential role in unintentionally creating team dependency.
- It connected recurring retrospective actions with the need for meaningful experiments and follow-up.
- It recognized that some problems may be systemic or organizational rather than problems the team can solve alone.
- It included valuable Scrum Master self-reflection questions.
- It maintained a coaching mindset based on curiosity rather than judgment.

## ⚠️ 6. What Needs Human Validation

- AI identified several possible causes for low participation, dependency, recurring retrospective actions, and cross-team coordination issues, but the Scrum Master needs to validate which causes actually apply to the team.
- Psychological safety, unresolved conflict, organizational barriers, and team dynamics cannot be reliably determined by AI without direct observation and conversation.
- The suggested coaching questions should be adapted to the team's situation rather than used as a scripted checklist.
- The Scrum Master needs to determine whether an issue is something the team can influence or whether it requires organizational intervention.
- The suggested progression from doing to stepping back should be adapted to the team's capability and context; the Scrum Master should not withdraw support simply to encourage independence.
- Improvement experiments should be selected by the team and evaluated using evidence from subsequent Sprints.
- The Scrum Master's own behavior should be inspected alongside the team's behavior to determine whether the Scrum Master is unintentionally reinforcing dependency.

## 🔴 7. Actual AI Weaknesses / Mistakes

No significant factual or Scrum-related errors were identified in the AI response.

The response appropriately avoided labeling the team as disengaged or incapable of self-management. It considered team, Scrum Master, and organizational factors and clearly distinguished coaching from simply solving problems for the team.

It also appropriately recognized that self-management does not mean the team must solve every problem without Scrum Master support.

The main limitation is contextual: AI can generate plausible causes and coaching approaches, but it cannot determine the team's actual psychological safety, organizational dynamics, relationships, or root causes without human observation and interaction.

## 💡 8. Key Learning

### 🌱 What I Learned From This Experiment

1. Coaching starts with **curiosity rather than judgment**.
2. The same team behavior can have very different underlying causes.
3. A Scrum Master should adapt between coaching, teaching, mentoring, and facilitation based on the situation.
4. Supporting the team does not mean solving every problem for them.
5. Self-management grows when the Scrum Master gradually shifts from **doing → supporting → coaching → stepping back**.
6. The Scrum Master's own behavior should be inspected because they may unintentionally create dependency.
7. Recurring improvement actions should become small experiments that can be inspected and adapted.
8. AI can help a Scrum Master prepare better questions, but the actual coaching relationship depends on human interaction, trust, observation, and context.

## 🚀 9. Final Scrum Master Approach

I would use ChatGPT as a **coaching preparation and reflection tool**, not as a replacement for the coaching relationship.

My approach would be:

1. Observe the team's behavior without immediately judging it.
2. Use ChatGPT to explore possible causes and coaching questions.
3. Validate those possibilities through conversations and observation.
4. Choose the appropriate approach—coaching, teaching, mentoring, or facilitation—based on the situation.
5. Encourage the team to take increasing ownership while providing support where genuinely needed.
6. Inspect whether my own behavior is unintentionally creating dependency.
7. Help the team select small, meaningful improvement experiments.
8. Inspect the results and adapt the approach in future Sprints.

### 🔑 Key Takeaway

> **A good Scrum Master doesn't simply solve the team's problems; they help the team become increasingly capable of solving problems themselves.**
